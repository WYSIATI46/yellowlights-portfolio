# The Most Cited Man in Law: A Portrait of Cass Sunstein

*He's the most frequently cited legal scholar in America. The real question is whether there's anything he hasn't written about.*

## Opening: A number named after him

There's a peculiar kind of fame reserved for scholars who are cited so often, by so many people, that someone eventually turns the citation itself into a joke. In 2007, the legal journal *The Green Bag* coined the "Sunstein number," a tongue-in-cheek measure of how many degrees of separation separated any legal academic from Cass Sunstein, an homage to the Erdő s number mathematicians use to track collaborative distance from a famously prolific colleague. By 2014, studies of legal citations found Sunstein to be the most frequently cited legal scholar in America, and it wasn't especially close.[^1]

The joke works because it's barely an exaggeration. Sunstein has published hundreds of articles and dozens of books, across constitutional law, administrative law, animal rights, conspiracy theories, Star Wars, and the psychology of everyday decision-making, and he has done it while holding down some of the most demanding jobs in American governance. The natural question isn't what Sunstein has written about. It's whether there's anything he hasn't.

## A teenager who fell out of love with Ayn Rand

Cass Robert Sunstein was born September 21, 1954, in Salem, Massachusetts, to a builder father and a schoolteacher mother.[^2][^1] As a teenager he went through a brief, intense infatuation with Ayn Rand, the kind of ideological crush that hits smart adolescents hard, before it curdled. "After about six weeks of enchantment," he later wrote, "her books started to make me sick. Contemptuous toward most of humanity, merciless about human frailty, and constantly hammering on the moral evils of redistribution, they produced a sense of claustrophobia."[^1]

It's a small biographical footnote, but it's also a preview of the entire intellectual project that would follow. Sunstein spent his career building elaborate, humane counterarguments to exactly the worldview Rand offered, one where the rugged individual owes nothing to anyone, where dependency is weakness, where government is an intrusion rather than a condition of freedom itself. He would later argue, with a straight face and considerable rigor, that Americans should "celebrate tax day," because without the courts, the banks, and the regulators that taxes fund, there would be no property to defend and no liberty worth having in the first place.[^1] The claustrophobia he felt reading Rand at sixteen never really left him; it just got a law degree.

## Harvard, twice, and a taste for winning arguments

Sunstein went to Harvard as an undergraduate, played on the varsity squash team, edited the *Harvard Lampoon*, and graduated magna cum laude in 1975 before going straight into Harvard Law School, where he sat on the winning team at the Ames Moot Court Competition and graduated magna cum laude again in 1978.[^1] He clerked for a Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court justice, then for Justice Thurgood Marshall on the U.S. Supreme Court, a formative apprenticeship under one of the century's great dissenters, before a short stint in the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel.[^1]

In 1981 he landed at the University of Chicago Law School, the same building where, a few years later, a young constitutional law lecturer named Barack Obama would become a colleague and, eventually, a president who'd call on him. Chicago made Sunstein, in the words of a fellow professor, "a Chicago person through and through," and it was there, layered under the free-market orthodoxy of Milton Friedman's economics department, that Sunstein built a parallel legal empire: books on free speech, judicial minimalism, environmental law, animal rights, arriving at a pace that made his colleagues joke about his productivity even as they cited him constantly.[^1]

## The unlikely partnership that made "nudge" a household word

Sometime in the mid-1990s, a new arrival at Chicago's business school named Richard Thaler found himself approached, almost immediately, by a law professor eager to talk. That was Sunstein, and the friendship that followed would eventually produce the most influential idea either of them ever put into the world.[^3] Where Thaler had spent two decades cataloging the ways real people depart from rational-actor models, Sunstein brought the machinery to turn those departures into policy, a legal scholar's instinct for translating psychological insight into rules, defaults, and design.

Their early joint paper on behavioral law and economics landed inside Chicago's law school like an uninvited opinion at a dinner party, tolerated, barely.[^3] It didn't matter. By 2008, Thaler and Sunstein had published *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness*, coining "choice architecture" and "libertarian paternalism," the idea that you could redesign defaults, retirement enrollment, organ donation, cafeteria layouts, to help people make better decisions without ever removing their freedom to choose otherwise.[^4][^5] Sunstein and Thaler called it "soft paternalism," a nudge rather than a shove, and the distinction mattered enormously to Sunstein, whose legal training made him unusually attentive to the difference between steering someone and coercing them.[^5]

The book found its most eager audience not in bookstores but in government. Barack Obama, Sunstein's former Chicago colleague, embraced it. So did British Prime Minister David Cameron, whose government stood up an entire Behavioural Insights Team built on the book's premise.[^1][^5] Nudging stopped being an academic curiosity and became, almost overnight, a genuine tool of statecraft.

## Becoming the thing he studied: four years inside the machine

In 2009, Obama appointed Sunstein to run the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, the obscure but formidable office that reviews nearly every significant federal regulation before it takes effect, essentially handing the country's leading theorist of nudges the actual machinery of nudging.[^1] It was not a quiet appointment. Progressive legal scholars and environmentalists raised alarms, worried that his instincts toward cost-benefit analysis and regulatory minimalism would blunt the very protections they wanted strengthened, and his confirmation stalled for months before the Senate finally voted him through, 57 to 40.[^1]

For four years, Sunstein applied behavioral economics from inside the building rather than writing about it from outside: streamlining rules, tightening fuel-economy standards, rethinking disclosure requirements, all with an eye toward what he called the least intrusive nudge that could still do real good.[^5] He left in 2012, but he never really returned to being purely an academic observer again. In 2021 he went back into government under Biden, at the Department of Homeland Security, working on resilience against flooding and extreme heat, and on cutting the administrative burdens, what he and Thaler would later call "sludge," that make government services harder to access than they should be, work that earned him the Department's Distinguished Public Service Medal in 2024.[^6]

## The scholar who wanted government to whisper, not shout

Underneath the sheer volume of Sunstein's output sits a fairly consistent temperament: a deep suspicion of anyone, judge or bureaucrat, who wants to make sweeping changes when a smaller, more careful move would do. He's built an entire judicial philosophy, minimalism, around the idea that courts should decide the case in front of them and resist the temptation to remake the law wholesale.[^1] Critics on the left have found him too accommodating of conservative jurists; critics on the right have called him a stealth big-government paternalist. Sunstein has mostly shrugged off both labels, more interested in the mechanics of a good outcome than in occupying a clean ideological box.

That same instinct, minimal intervention, maximal care, got him into serious trouble in one notable case. A 2008 paper he co-wrote proposing "cognitive infiltration" of extremist conspiracy-theory groups, floating the idea of government agents entering online forums to seed doubt about false narratives, drew sharp, sustained criticism from civil libertarians who saw it as uncomfortably close to state propaganda.[^1] It's a strange footnote for a scholar so consistently focused on nudges rather than shoves, though Sunstein would likely argue the two ideas share a logic: influence people's beliefs gently, from the inside, rather than banning or punishing the beliefs directly.

## A life reorganized by love, twice

Sunstein's personal life has its own quiet narrative of reinvention. He was married first to Lisa Ruddick, an English professor he'd met as a Harvard undergraduate; the marriage ended in divorce, and their daughter Ellyn grew up to become a journalist and photographer.[^1] He then spent nearly a decade with philosopher Martha Nussbaum, a relationship serious enough that when Senator Saxby Chambliss later blocked Sunstein's regulatory appointment, the controversy centered partly on a book Sunstein had co-edited with Nussbaum on animal rights.[^1]

In 2008, he married Samantha Power, the war correspondent turned diplomat whom he'd met while both were advising Obama's presidential campaign. They wed on the Fourth of July, in a church in County Kerry, Ireland, and went on to have two children together while Power herself served as U.S. Ambassador to the United Nations under Obama, and later as USAID Administrator under Biden.[^1] It's an unusually public marriage for two people who each, in their own domains, spent careers thinking hard about how institutions should exercise power responsibly, one through law and psychology, the other through diplomacy and moral witness.

Off the clock, Sunstein plays squash with genuine seriousness — national-caliber in his prep school years, still competitive decades later — a detail that fits a man whose entire intellectual career has been a series of fast, precise exchanges.[^1]

## Where the next volume is headed

Sunstein shows no signs of slowing his output, and if anything, the themes he's circling now feel more urgent than the ones that made him famous. In 2021, he co-authored *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* with Daniel Kahneman and Olivier Sibony, turning his legal eye toward the unwanted variability in professional judgment, why two judges hand down wildly different sentences for identical crimes, why two doctors reach different diagnoses from the same scan, and how structured decision protocols might close that gap.[^1]

He's also become one of the most persistent voices tracking "sludge," his and Thaler's term for the friction, paperwork, and administrative burden that quietly erode the good a nudge is supposed to do, a concern that clearly followed him into his DHS work on cutting red tape around disaster relief.[^6] Given his history of showing up wherever government meets behavioral science, it wouldn't be surprising to see him drawn next toward AI governance and algorithmic decision-making, territory that combines his two oldest obsessions: how institutions should regulate powerful new tools, and how the humans running those institutions predictably get things wrong. He's also continued co-teaching a Supreme Court seminar at Harvard alongside retired Justice Stephen Breyer, suggesting that whatever comes next, he intends to keep training the next generation of lawyers to think the way he does: carefully, incrementally, and with an eye always on the smaller, better move.[^1]
