# The Man Who Explained Us to Ourselves: A Portrait of Michael Lewis

*A review he didn't like led him, eight years and one unlikely friendship later, to write the truest book of his career.*

## Opening: A review that stung

Most writers get bad reviews and move on. Michael Lewis got a review of *Moneyball* in 2003 that didn't attack his prose or his facts, but told him he didn't fully understand his own book, and he couldn't shake it for years. The review came from an economist named Richard Thaler and a legal scholar named Cass Sunstein, and their complaint was oddly specific: the scouting biases Lewis had spent a whole book documenting, the tendency of baseball insiders to overvalue a player's jawline and undervalue his on-base percentage, weren't just baseball quirks. They were cognitive biases, first identified decades earlier by two Israeli psychologists named Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman.[^1] Lewis had never heard either name. "I thought, 'My God, how'd I miss that?'" he later admitted. "I never asked the next question, why? What's going on in the mind? It hadn't even occurred to me that anybody ever had."[^1]

That gap between what Lewis had shown and what he hadn't yet understood would take him nearly a decade to close. What he built in that decade wasn't just another bestseller. It became, by wide critical consensus, the best book of a career that had already produced several genuine classics, and it clarified something that had been true of Lewis's work all along without anyone quite naming it this way: he had spent twenty years unconsciously practicing behavioral economics before he ever knew the field existed.

## An accidental art historian

Michael Monroe Lewis was born October 15, 1960, in New Orleans, the son of a corporate attorney and a community activist, and descended, on his father's side, from an early-nineteenth-century Louisiana judge.[^2] None of that pointed toward financial journalism. At Princeton, Lewis majored in art and archaeology, graduating cum laude in 1982 after writing a 166-page senior thesis titled "Donatello and the Antique," and by his own account genuinely intended to become an art historian until he realized, with some dismay, that the job market for Renaissance sculpture scholars was approximately as small as the subject itself.[^2] He briefly apprenticed with the New York art dealer Daniel Wildenstein before pivoting, almost by accident, into a master's in economics at the London School of Economics, a degree he has described less as a calling than as a plausible-sounding thing to do next.[^2]

That indirection matters, because it explains something structural about how Lewis writes. He isn't a finance obsessive who discovered narrative. He's a storyteller who discovered finance was full of stories nobody else was bothering to tell well. He was hired by Salomon Brothers in the mid-1980s, trained in New York, then shipped to London as a bond salesman, and it was there, reading *The Economist* and *The Wall Street Journal* for pleasure rather than duty, that he began to suspect journalism, not trading, was the job he actually wanted.[^2] The strange, gladiatorial culture he witnessed on Salomon's trading floor became *Liar's Poker* (1989), the book that put him on the map and established, in its first pages, the sensibility he'd spend the next thirty-five years refining: equal parts anthropologist and satirist, always more interested in the human beings distorting a system than in the system's mechanics for their own sake.

## The pattern, restated

From *Liar's Poker* onward, the same move repeats with almost eerie consistency. *The New New Thing* (1999) found it in the manic self-mythologizing of Silicon Valley. *Moneyball* (2003), later a film with Brad Pitt, found it in a small-market baseball team that got good by ignoring what scouts thought they knew, a thesis pointed enough that a *Forbes* critic later credited Lewis with a rare talent: walking into territory "already mined by hundreds of writers" and finding gems everyone else had missed.[^2] *The Blind Side* (2006), adapted into a 2009 film that won Sandra Bullock an Academy Award, found it in the peculiar economics of a single offensive-line position nobody outside football front offices had ever thought to value properly. *The Big Short* (2010), whose 2015 film adaptation won an Oscar for Adapted Screenplay, found it in the handful of traders who read the housing market correctly while nearly everyone paid to understand risk read it wrong.[^2][^3] *Flash Boys* (2014) found it in high-frequency trading, and became contentious enough that then-SEC observers publicly disputed its central claim that markets were "rigged," a dispute the SEC itself partially validated months later by launching a new review of market structure.[^2] *The Fifth Risk* (2018) found it in the unglamorous federal agencies nobody bothers to understand until the people running them stop caring whether they fail. Lewis has said flatly that *Moneyball* was, underneath the baseball, "about the way markets misvalue people,"[^1] and that single sentence turns out to describe nearly everything he has ever published, whether the mispriced asset was a shortstop, a mortgage bond, a tackle, or, eventually, an idea that had been sitting in academic journals for thirty years waiting for someone to translate it.

## The question he didn't know to ask

What makes the Thaler-Sunstein review sting in hindsight is that Lewis's entire method had always been to find the human beings hiding behind an inefficiency and let them explain it. He'd built a career on the premise that expert judgment fails in patterned, discoverable ways, without realizing two psychologists had already proven that premise, mathematically and repeatedly, thirty years before he set foot on a baseball diamond.[^1] It took him "a few years after that review to finally have the wits to call Danny Kahneman," Lewis has said, and what he discovered when he finally did was almost absurdly convenient: Kahneman spent his summers in the hills directly above Lewis's own home in Berkeley.[^1]

They had coffee. The coffee became long walks. The walks became a story Lewis hadn't gone looking for, about what he called "a passionate kind of love affair without sex" between two men who "were more attached to each other than they were to anybody else in their lives."[^1] Somewhere in those hills, a project that began as an attempt to fill in a footnote he'd missed turned into the book Lewis would later describe as possibly his best.

## Eight years, and everything that could have been

It took Lewis roughly eight years to write *The Undoing Project*, by his own accounting, a span during which a writer of his commercial standing could easily have produced two or three more surefire bestsellers instead.[^1] The opportunity cost is worth naming plainly: this is a man whose books routinely become studio films with A-list casts, choosing instead to spend most of a decade reconstructing the emotional interior of a scientific partnership that most of his readership had never heard of, that offered no heist, no market crash, no clean villain, and that resolved, when he started, in a friendship that had already ended in death.

Part of the delay was structural and unavoidable. Tversky had died of melanoma in 1996, twenty years before the book's 2016 publication, which meant Lewis had to reconstruct half of his central relationship entirely from memory, correspondence, and the testimony of people who'd loved a man they'd lost decades earlier.[^1] Lewis expected this to be the hardest part of the project. It wasn't. "Amos is so vivid," he said. "He was such a strong personality... It wasn't that hard to re-create him. He's such a great character."[^1] The harder work turned out to be something quieter and slower: earning, walk after walk, season after season, the trust of a notoriously private survivor willing to hand over the texture of a grief he'd been carrying, largely alone, for two decades.

## Two men, rendered whole

What Lewis eventually delivered is a case study in his actual gift, which has never really been explaining ideas so much as making readers fall for the people who had them. He's said as much directly: "I knew that if I just got their words and their behavior down on the page, their characters would infect people's minds."[^1] The anecdotes he collected do exactly that, and they're worth dwelling on because they show the craft at close range rather than in summary. There's Tversky at a party, mistaken by an actual, prize-winning physicist for the smartest physicist in the room, a mix-up the physicist could not quite bring himself to believe once corrected.[^1] There's the office comparison Lewis leaned on to dramatize the whole architecture of the friendship: Tversky's desk bare enough to hold a single centered pencil, a monument to a mind that discarded anything nonessential, against Kahneman's chaos, so total that his own secretary eventually tied his scissors to his chair leg just to stop him from losing them.[^1] Felix and Oscar, reconceived as the two men who invented behavioral economics between them.

And there's the detail Lewis returns to almost sheepishly, about how Kahneman's insight into regression to the mean, drawn originally from Israeli Air Force flight instructors convinced that criticism improved performance better than praise, quietly changed how Lewis coaches his own children's sports teams decades later. "It's baloney," he concluded of his own long-held instincts. "It's an illusion."[^1] It's a small confession, but it says something large about why the book cost him eight years: Lewis wasn't reporting on cognitive bias from a safe journalistic distance. He was catching himself inside it, in real time, on a Saturday-morning sideline, and deciding the honest thing to do was put that admission on the page rather than keep it for himself.

## A prequel he didn't know he needed to write

Lewis has described *The Undoing Project* as, in effect, "a kind of prequel to *Moneyball*," the origin story of the very insight that made his earlier book possible without his fully understanding it at the time.[^1] That's a modest way of describing what the book actually accomplishes. Kahneman and Tversky's research, conducted largely in what Lewis calls "arcane, boring psychology journals" through the 1970s, had already won a Nobel Prize, already been cited thousands of times, already reshaped economics, medicine, and law from the inside out.[^1] What it hadn't done, in three decades of academic triumph, was become legible to the person paying for a loved one's cancer treatment, or coaching a Little League team, or reading a headline about a stock market panic and wondering why intelligent, well-paid people keep making the same mistake twice.

That's the specific gap Lewis filled, and it's worth being precise about the mechanism. Academic psychology had proven that people mistake a coherent story for an accurate one, that losing something hurts more than gaining its equivalent feels good, that expert intuition fails predictably outside a narrow band of conditions. Lewis didn't add a new finding to that list. He proved the underlying thesis a second time, from the outside, by writing a friendship vivid enough that readers who would never open a *Journal of Experimental Psychology* walked away understanding loss aversion anyway, because they'd watched it operate inside two men they'd grown to genuinely care about.

## A borrowed craft, honestly acknowledged

Lewis didn't invent this method from nothing, and to his credit he's never pretended otherwise. He has written openly about his debt to Tom Wolfe, the New Journalism pioneer whose techniques, scene-by-scene construction, extended dialogue, deep point-of-view immersion in a character's interior life, Lewis absorbed and redeployed on finance and science rather than on astronauts and socialites. His 2015 *Vanity Fair* profile "How Tom Wolfe Became... Tom Wolfe" became, fittingly, the basis for a 2023 documentary, *Radical Wolfe*.[^2] Wolfe's own account of the New Journalism describes exactly the toolkit Lewis deploys on every page of *The Undoing Project*: real scenes instead of abstracted historical summary, dialogue reconstructed from exhaustive legwork, and a refusal to let factual rigor crowd out narrative drive.[^4] Lewis's genuine innovation was pointing that toolkit at subjects Wolfe never touched, derivatives desks, sabermetrics, cognitive psychology, and discovering it worked just as well on a spreadsheet as it did on a test pilot.

## The reception, and the doubts worth naming honestly

Critics largely agreed Lewis had pulled off something rare with *The Undoing Project*. Glenn Altschuler, reviewing it for the *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette*, called it possibly Lewis's best work outright,[^2] and the broader critical reception was warm enough to make the eight-year gamble look, in retrospect, obviously worthwhile. That's not the whole record, though, and a fair portrait of Lewis shouldn't pretend his method comes without real cost. The same instinct for narrative momentum that makes *Moneyball* thrilling drew sharp criticism from sportswriter Allen Barra, who argued in *The Atlantic* that Lewis had simply gotten baseball history wrong, claiming the sport was less competitively balanced than it had ever been at precisely the moment it was more balanced than ever.[^2] *Flash Boys* provoked a public rebuttal from then-SEC Chair Mary Jo White, who told Congress flatly that "the markets are not rigged."[^2] And *Going Infinite* (2023), Lewis's account of Sam Bankman-Fried written while FTX was actively collapsing around him, drew the sharpest criticism of his career: journalist Michael Hiltzik dismissed the surrounding hype as "a torrent of nonsense," and *The New York Times* wrote that Lewis's extensive access had given him "a front-row seat, from which he could apparently see nothing."[^2] Even sympathetic critics hedged; *The New Yorker* called the book "stupefyingly pleasurable" while predicting it might be remembered as either "the pinnacle or the nadir" of his career.[^2] It's a fair tension to hold honestly: the empathy that makes Lewis's subjects feel fully human, worth your evening, worth defending, is the same empathy that can tip, when the subject is less trustworthy than Kahneman or Tversky, into something closer to credulity. *The Undoing Project* is the case, by near-universal agreement, where that instinct found its ideal subject and nothing distorted the picture.

## What grief taught him he already knew

In May 2021, Lewis's nineteen-year-old daughter Dixie was killed alongside her boyfriend, Ross Schultz, when the car he was driving crossed a double yellow line on a Northern California highway and struck an oncoming truck head-on; investigators never determined why.[^5][^6] Lewis has said, with characteristic bluntness, that he nearly stopped writing altogether.[^7] What he's said since carries an uncomfortable echo of the very ideas that made him famous. Asked to explain a crash with no alcohol, no clear mechanical failure, and no satisfying villain, Lewis and his family were left facing exactly the kind of event Kahneman and Tversky spent careers studying: a random, meaningless outcome that the human mind, desperate for a coherent story, keeps trying and failing to explain.[^6][^8] He has spoken of Dixie in terms that sound less like a father eulogizing a child than a writer describing a character he wished he'd had more time with: "She was an extraordinarily brave person, like, beyond belief brave. She made big sacrifices for a future she saw. And it was heartbreaking that she didn't get that future."[^7] He dedicated his next book to her.[^7] It's a private tragedy, and it would be wrong to lean on it too hard here. But it's worth noting, gently, that the man who spent a decade explaining how the mind manufactures false certainty out of randomness was, in his own life, forced to sit inside a random tragedy with no story that would ever quite resolve it, and chose, eventually, to keep writing anyway.

## Where the work has landed since

Lewis hasn't slowed down, even through that loss. He pivoted into podcasting with *Against the Rules* in 2019, a series built, fittingly, around the largely invisible people entrusted to keep a system fair, referees, art authenticators, regulators, produced through Pushkin Industries, the company founded by Malcolm Gladwell and Jacob Weisberg.[^2] The subject is a natural extension of everything before it: another hidden mechanism nobody thinks to examine until it fails them, told this time in a medium where, as Lewis has said of the format's particular gift, "you can hear the characters' voices," and a source's sincerity "just jumps off the tape" in a way prose has to work much harder to earn.

Asked once whether it surprised him how thoroughly Kahneman and Tversky's ideas had seeped into fields with no obvious connection to psychology, medicine, the military, professional sports officiating, Lewis's answer doubled as a fair description of his own life's work: "What they identified were essentially the tricks the mind plays on itself... Danny and Amos were showing the way the mind, when it's telling stories to resolve uncertainty, makes mistakes. It does apply to everything. Everything goes through the mind."[^1]

## The takeaway

There's a reason it doesn't feel like much of a stretch to reach for Twain when trying to place Michael Lewis in American letters, even knowing the comparison can't be pushed too far. Not because the two men sound alike on the page, they don't, but because they share something rarer than style: an ability to take whatever the culture has decided is too technical, too remote, or too boring for a curious person's attention, riverboat piloting, bond trading, sabermetrics, the tacit machinery of human judgment, and hand it back as a story an ordinary reader can't put down and never feels condescended to for finishing. Lewis has never needed to write down to his audience, because his actual gift has always been finding the plain, human sentence hiding inside the technical one, and trusting that the sentence, told honestly, will do the rest of the work itself. That gift held steady across market crashes and Silicon Valley booms and football sidelines. It held, by his own account, even through the hardest thing he's ever had to write his way past, the death of his own daughter, in a story with no moral and no explanation, the exact kind of story his own books had spent decades warning us the mind will always try, and fail, to make sense of anyway.

Academic psychology spent thirty years proving that human judgment fails in specific, discoverable, correctable ways, and it took a former bond salesman with an art history degree, walking through the Berkeley hills with a man he'd only just met, to make the rest of us actually feel it in our own lives rather than merely believe it as a fact about someone else's. If Kahneman and Tversky showed the world what was distorted in the mirror, Lewis is the one who got enough people to actually look into it, not with shame, but with something closer to recognition, and maybe even relief, at finally understanding what had been staring back at them the whole time.
