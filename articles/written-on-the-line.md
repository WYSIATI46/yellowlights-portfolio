# Written on the Line: A History of Naturalistic Decision-Making

*They stopped trusting the lab and started watching firefighters instead. Four decades later, it changed how armies, hospitals, and cockpits train their people.*

## Opening: The workshop nobody wanted to attend

In 1989, the U.S. Army Research Institute had a problem nobody in the psychology establishment particularly wanted to solve. For decades, the reigning model of decision-making, the one taught in business schools and baked into military doctrine, assumed that a good decision-maker generates a set of options, weighs the costs and benefits of each, and selects the one with the highest expected value. It's an elegant model. It is also, as an Army psychologist named Judith Orasanu had begun to suspect, almost completely disconnected from how anyone actually makes decisions when the building is on fire, the enemy is close, or the patient's blood pressure is crashing.[^1][^2]

Orasanu, working out of the Army Research Institute's Basic Research group, had assembled a small cadre of researchers who shared her unease: Gary Klein, Marvin Cohen, David Noble, Raanan Lipshitz.[^2] None of them were household names, and none of them were trying to overturn Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases program, which by the late 1980s already dominated the psychology of judgment. They were simply troubled by a narrower, more practical question: soldiers, firefighters, pilots, and nurses were making life-or-death calls every day, under conditions no laboratory could ethically or practically replicate, time pressure, ambiguous information, high stakes, exhaustion, and they seemed to be doing it well, using something that didn't look like formal option comparison at all. In 1989, this small group convened a workshop in Dayton, Ohio to compare notes. It produced a book, *Decision Making in Action*, and, almost without anyone declaring it so at the time, a new field.[^3][^4]

They called it Naturalistic Decision Making. The name itself was a quiet act of defiance: naturalistic, as opposed to laboratory-based; decision making, not decision theory. It was a field built by people who had grown suspicious of the gambling tasks and abstract urn problems that had generated most of what psychology thought it knew about human judgment, and who wanted to go watch the real thing instead.

## The man who started watching firefighters

If NDM has a founder, it's Gary Klein, and his path to the field was almost accidental. Klein was born in New York City on February 5, 1944, earned his psychology degree from City College of New York in 1964, and his PhD in experimental psychology from the University of Pittsburgh in 1969.[^4] He spent the first phase of his career doing fairly conventional academic psychology at Oakland University in Michigan and Wilberforce University in Ohio, the kind of work that could have led to an entirely unremarkable career studying whatever laboratory phenomena happened to be fashionable at the time.

The turn came in 1974, when Klein joined the U.S. Air Force as a research psychologist. The 1973 Arab oil embargo had made jet fuel prohibitively expensive for routine training flights, forcing the Air Force to shift much of its pilot training into flight simulators, and suddenly there was real institutional money and motivation behind understanding how people actually develop expertise under realistic, high-fidelity conditions rather than in a psych lab with a keyboard and a screen.[^4] Klein spent four years steeped in that problem, and in 1978 he struck out on his own, founding a small research and development company called Klein Associates specifically to study what would later be formalized as Naturalistic Decision Making. It grew, slowly, from a one-man operation to thirty-seven employees before he sold it to Applied Research Associates in 2005.[^4]

The study that made his reputation, and gave NDM its first genuinely startling empirical result, came in 1985, when Klein and colleagues Roberta Calderwood and Anne Clinton-Cirocco went into the field to study fireground commanders, the officers who arrive at a burning building and have to decide, in seconds, how to fight it.[^4] The standard model of decision-making predicted that these commanders would rapidly generate several possible courses of action and compare them. That is not remotely what Klein found. In the vast majority of the decision points his team studied, experienced commanders didn't compare options at all. They looked at the fire, recognized it as a version of something they'd encountered before, and moved almost immediately to a single course of action, testing it not against alternatives but against a rapid mental simulation of how it would likely play out, adjusting only if that simulation revealed an obvious flaw.[^5] Klein called this the Recognition-Primed Decision model, RPD, and it became the founding empirical result of the entire NDM tradition.[^6][^3]

It's worth sitting with how radical this was in 1985. The dominant view in decision science, then as now in many quarters, held that expert judgment, when it departs from formal analysis, is doing so because of cognitive limitation, not competence. Klein's fireground commanders weren't failing to analyze their options because they were too rushed or too tired to think properly. They were succeeding by not analyzing options at all, replacing comparison with recognition, a completely different cognitive strategy that Klein argued was often superior in genuinely time-pressured, high-stakes domains.[^5]

## A movement, not just a model

What happened after 1989 is easy to undersell if you only know Klein's name. The Dayton workshop became a recurring event, an NDM conference roughly every two years, moving between the United States and Europe: Dayton, Aberdeen, Warrenton, Stockholm, Pensacola, Amsterdam, Pacific Grove, and London, and onward through Orlando, Marseille, Washington, Bath, San Francisco, and Toulouse, right up through 2022.[^2] In 1995, the field institutionalized itself further with the founding of the Cognitive Engineering and Decision Making Technical Group, which grew into one of the largest technical groups within the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society, a sign that this was no longer a boutique interest of a handful of Army-funded researchers but a genuine, sustained research community.[^1]

The intellectual contributors multiplied alongside the conferences. Judith Orasanu, the Army researcher who convened the original workshop, went on to study decision-making in aviation cockpits and became one of the field's most cited voices on team decision-making under stress. Raanan Lipshitz brought organizational and managerial applications into the fold. And running alongside NDM, sometimes overlapping with it and sometimes standing apart, was Gerd Gigerenzer's program on fast-and-frugal heuristics and ecological rationality, which supplied much of the formal, mathematical backbone for the claim that simple, information-light decision rules could outperform complex models precisely because they were tuned to the statistical structure of real environments. Gigerenzer wasn't an NDM researcher in Klein's original sense, he worked more from mathematical modeling than fieldwork, but the two programs became close intellectual cousins, united by a shared skepticism toward the idea that more information and more deliberation always produce better decisions.

By 2003, the field had grown broad enough that Klein and colleagues introduced the term "macrocognition" to describe an expanded version of NDM's agenda, one that folded in sensemaking, planning under ill-defined goals, and team coordination, subjects that a narrower focus on individual recognition-primed decisions couldn't fully capture.[^7] It was less a rebranding than an acknowledgment that the questions the field had started asking about individual firefighters had grown into questions about how entire teams and organizations think together under pressure.

## The tools that came out of the fires

NDM's contribution was never just a theory of what experts do. It was also a growing toolkit for training and evaluating decision-making, largely because the researchers involved kept ending up embedded inside the very organizations, military, medical, industrial, whose decisions they were studying.

Klein developed the Critical Decision Method, a structured interview technique for eliciting the tacit, often unverbalized knowledge experts use without realizing they're using it, the kind of knowledge that doesn't show up if you simply ask someone "how did you know what to do?" and expect a clean answer.[^4] In 1998, he introduced a deceptively simple risk-assessment tool called the premortem: rather than asking a team to imagine a project succeeding, you ask them to imagine, in vivid detail, that it has already failed, and then work backward to explain why. It's a small inversion, but a powerful one, because it gives permission to voice doubts and objections that groupthink and social pressure normally suppress before a plan is finalized.[^8] Kahneman himself later called it one of the best de-biasing tools he'd ever encountered, a rare instance of an NDM technique earning explicit praise from the leader of the rival camp.[^8]

Klein's RPD research fed directly into military doctrine, reshaping how the U.S. Army and Marine Corps trained officers to make command decisions, moving away from an exclusively option-comparison model of tactical decision-making and toward frameworks that explicitly cultivated pattern recognition and rapid mental simulation.[^4] Later in his career, Klein helped lead the team that redesigned the White House Situation Room, and in 2015 he founded ShadowBox, a company built around a scenario-based training method, developed originally from a fire battalion chief's graduate thesis, designed to let trainees essentially borrow an expert's way of seeing a situation without needing the expert physically present.[^4]

Klein's own books trace the field's maturing self-understanding for a broader audience: *Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions* (1999) laid out RPD and the fireground findings for a general readership, *The Power of Intuition* (2004) translated the research into workplace advice, and *Streetlights and Shadows: Searching for the Keys to Adaptive Decision Making* (2009) took a more skeptical turn, examining where naturalistic approaches themselves fall short. *Seeing What Others Don't* (2013) shifted focus to insight and creative breakthroughs, a natural extension of a career spent studying the moment when experience suddenly clicks into recognition.[^4]

## When it worked: two fires, decades apart

The clearest way to understand what NDM captures, and what standard decision models miss, is to look at real high-stakes situations where recognition-primed judgment either saved lives or, when it broke down, cost them.

Consider the difference between a well-calibrated fireground commander and what happens when the underlying conditions for recognition-primed expertise are absent. Klein's own fireground study is itself the positive case: commanders who had spent years accumulating direct, fast-feedback experience with fires of every kind could look at smoke color, structural behavior, and building layout and generate, almost instantly, a course of action that would have taken a formal decision matrix many precious minutes to construct, minutes a burning building rarely grants.[^5] This wasn't luck or bravado. It was exactly the kind of learned, pattern-based expertise that Kahneman and Klein later agreed requires a "kind," learnable environment with fast feedback, precisely the conditions a career firefighter's daily working life provides in abundance.[^9]

The tragic counter-case, one NDM researchers themselves have studied closely, is the 1949 Mann Gulch fire in Montana, in which a team of smokejumpers was overtaken by a sudden, explosive wildfire, killing thirteen firefighters. Organizational scholar Karl Weick's landmark analysis of Mann Gulch, later engaged with directly by Klein, treated the disaster as a case of "collapse of sensemaking," where the crew's mental model of the fire's behavior and their own situation became catastrophically disconnected from a reality that was changing faster than their frame for understanding it could keep pace. The foreman, Wagner Dodge, actually improvised something close to a recognition-primed insight in the disaster's final minutes, lighting an escape fire that burned away fuel in a patch of ground he then lay down in, a move that saved his own life but that his crew, lacking the same tacit understanding, didn't trust or follow, and most died as a result. It's a haunting illustration of exactly what Klein's framework predicts: recognition-primed intuition can produce genuinely superior, life-saving judgment, but only for the person whose accumulated experience generated it, and only when others around them have enough shared understanding to recognize a good call when they see one.

## When it failed: the USS Vincennes

If Mann Gulch shows what happens when a team's sensemaking collapses under a genuinely novel threat, the 1988 USS Vincennes incident shows the opposite failure mode, one that NDM researchers have studied precisely because it looks superficially like recognition-primed decision-making but reveals its dark twin.

On July 3, 1988, the USS Vincennes, a Navy guided-missile cruiser operating in the Persian Gulf during heightened tensions with Iran, detected an aircraft climbing from Bandar Abbas airport and tracked it as it approached the ship. Under enormous time pressure, amid recent memory of an actual Iranian attack pattern and the trauma of the USS Stark's own crippling missile strike months earlier, the crew's combat information center classified the contact as a hostile, descending F-14 fighter jet and launched two missiles, destroying the aircraft. It was Iran Air Flight 655, a civilian Airbus climbing on a scheduled route, killing all 290 people aboard.[^10]

The subsequent Department of Defense investigation found something psychologically unsettling: the crew hadn't been careless or malicious, they had, under extreme stress, pattern-matched the ambiguous radar signature to a threat scenario they had rehearsed and feared, a hostile fighter jet, rather than to the actual, more mundane reality of a climbing commercial airliner on a known flight path.[^10] This is recognition-primed decision-making functioning exactly as the model predicts it will, and exactly as Kahneman would later warn it can, when the underlying conditions for trustworthy expertise are absent. The crew's prior experience had trained them on a narrow, threat-saturated pattern library shaped by recent trauma, not the broad, well-calibrated library of a fireground commander with years of representative, fast-feedback exposure to the full range of situations they might face. It was, in the language Kahneman and Klein would later formalize, recognition operating inside a genuinely "wicked" environment, ambiguous radar data, compressed time, a recent history of real attacks, that made confident, fluent pattern-matching actively dangerous rather than reliably skilled.[^9] The tragedy became one of the most studied case examples in the NDM literature precisely because it demonstrates, in the starkest possible terms, that recognition-primed judgment is not an unconditional virtue. It's only as good as the pattern library and the environment that built it.

## The field's own reckoning

To Klein's credit, and to the field's, NDM has never presented itself as a movement claiming intuition is always superior to analysis. Klein's 2009 book *Streetlights and Shadows* is explicitly a book of second thoughts, an extended meditation on where naturalistic approaches themselves run into trouble: the difficulty of teaching tacit expertise explicitly, the danger of experts becoming overconfident precisely because their intuitions have worked so often before, the genuine limits of pattern recognition in situations that are truly unprecedented rather than merely unfamiliar.[^4] This kind of self-scrutiny is part of what made the 2009 collaboration with Kahneman possible in the first place, an NDM tradition willing to name its own failure modes had far more common ground with the heuristics-and-biases tradition than either side's public reputation suggested.[^1]

## Where the field is headed

NDM today looks less like an insurgent challenger to mainstream decision science and more like an established, quietly influential discipline that has simply moved into the infrastructure of high-stakes professions without needing a bestseller to get there. Its methods, the Critical Decision Method, cognitive task analysis, ShadowBox scenario training, are now standard tools in military, medical, and emergency-response training programs worldwide, even when the people using them have never heard the term "naturalistic decision making."[^4][^7] The field's most active current frontier, fittingly, is the same one reshaping every other corner of decision science: artificial intelligence. Klein and his colleagues have turned their attention to what they call the Artificial Intelligence Quotient, developing tools to help human operators build accurate mental models of the AI systems they now have to manage and trust, or distrust, in real time, an almost direct descendant of the original question that started the whole field: how do you know when a fast, fluent judgment, human or now algorithmic, actually deserves your confidence.[^4]

That question, more than any specific finding about firefighters or fighter pilots, is NDM's real and lasting contribution. Kahneman and Tversky taught the world to be suspicious of its own confidence. Klein, Orasanu, Gigerenzer, and the NDM tradition taught it something harder to compress into a single clever puzzle, but arguably just as important: that confidence built on genuine, well-fed, fast-feedback experience is not an illusion to be debunked, it's a real and measurable form of expertise, and learning to tell the two apart, the fireground commander's earned intuition from the Vincennes crew's trauma-shaped pattern-match, may be the single most consequential decision-making skill there is.
